Thermochimica for Molten Salts

This session uses Thermochimica with the MSTDB-TC version 4.1 fluoride database. It computes the equilibrium speciation, redox state, corrosion behavior, and off-gas composition of a molten fluoride salt. Every number in this deck comes from a calculation run on a stated input file.

Scope: standalone Thermochimica. A separate session covers the MOOSE coupling.

Segment 1

What the calculation does

  • Thermochimica computes the equilibrium state of a chemical system.
  • The equilibrium state is the arrangement of phases and species that gives the system the lowest possible Gibbs energy, at a given temperature and pressure, under the element mass balance.
  • The element mass balance is the rule that the total moles of each chemical element do not change during the calculation.
  • The program takes three inputs: temperature, pressure, and the mass balance of each element. It returns the stable phases, the amount of each, and the speciation inside each phase.
dG = 0 at fixed T, P, and total moles of each element

G is the Gibbs energy of the system. The solver finds phase and species amounts that drive dG as close to zero as its tolerance allows. The program reports a functional norm that states how close dG is to zero at the solution. A smaller functional norm is a tighter solution.

Segment 1

Elements in. Phases and speciation out.

  • The input to the program is a list of elements, not compounds. The program decides, on its own, what species form.
  • For a molten salt, Thermochimica reports the liquid phase with a quasichemical model. This model groups two cations and two anions into one unit, called a quadruplet.
  • In this phase, the program reports speciation as cation mole fractions, anion mole fractions, and quadruplet mole fractions. It does not report a simple list of molecules, the way an ideal gas phase does.
  • This is an equilibrium calculation. It does not compute a reaction rate or the speed at which a species moves between phases. It computes the end state and the driving force toward that end state.
  • A transport model needs this end state as an input. That model, and its coupling to Thermochimica, is the subject of a separate session.

Segment 3

Element chemical potentials in a two-species system

  • The LiF-BeF₂ liquid computed in Segment 2 has two independent species: LiF and BeF₂. The system has three elements: lithium, beryllium, and fluorine.
  • Two species cannot fix three independent element chemical potentials. The system has one fewer independent equation than it has elements. This condition is called an under-determined system.
  • An under-determined system gives one correct value for the Gibbs energy of the whole system, and one correct value for the chemical potential of each species. It does not give one correct value for the chemical potential of each element, taken alone.

Source: Piro, Poschmann, and Bajpai, Journal of Nuclear Materials, volume 526, page 151756, 2019.

Segment 3

Why the three values move together

μ(LiF) = μ_Li + μ_F μ(BeF₂) = μ_Be + 2·μ_F

For μ(LiF) and μ(BeF₂) to stay fixed while μ_F changes by an amount Δμ_F, the other two element potentials must change to cancel that amount exactly.

μ_Li must change by −Δμ_F to hold μ_Li + μ_F fixed μ_Be must change by −2·Δμ_F to hold μ_Be + 2·μ_F fixed

Segment 3

The rule for a stoichiometric salt

Runμ(LiF) = μ_Li + μ_Fμ(BeF₂) = μ_Be + 2μ_F
1. Exact stoichiometry−672.14 kJ/mol−1122.87 kJ/mol
2. Perturbed−672.10 kJ/mol−1122.95 kJ/mol
3. Fuzzy stoichiometry−672.10 kJ/mol−1122.95 kJ/mol

All three runs agree to under 0.1 kJ/mol.

Rule: do not report or use the chemical potential of a single element from a calculation on a stoichiometric molten salt. Report or use the chemical potential of a species instead.

If an element chemical potential is required, remove the under-determined condition first, by one of three methods:

  1. Change one input mass away from the exact stoichiometric ratio.
  2. Set fuzzy = .TRUE. in the input file. This option adds a small random change, by default ±1×10⁻¹² mole, to each nonzero entry of the stoichiometry matrix before the solver runs.
  3. Use a database with an explicit vacancy species on the relevant sublattice.

Caution: the fuzzy stoichiometry option can place a small, non-physical amount of an extra phase into the result. Check the phase assemblage after use.

Segment 4

Redox state from the fluorine mass balance

The next three runs hold lithium, beryllium, and uranium fixed. Only the fluorine mass balance changes. UF₃ needs three fluorine atoms for each uranium atom. UF₄ needs four. The fluorine remaining after lithium and beryllium take their share sets the mean oxidation number of uranium.

v̄ = (n_F − n_Li − 2·n_Be) / n_U

v̄ is the mean oxidation number of uranium: the oxidation number of uranium, averaged over every form of uranium present, weighted by the amount of each form. With n_Li = 66 mol and n_Be = 33 mol, lithium and beryllium consume 66 + 2×33 = 132 mol of fluorine as LiF and BeF₂. The remaining fluorine goes to uranium.

The computed mean oxidation number matches the mass balance ratio set on input, to four decimal places or better in every run. The fluorine mass balance chosen on input is the redox state of the uranium on output. This is a direct, checkable identity, not an approximation.

Segment 4

The equilibrium constant behind the ratio

UF4 (in melt) ⇌ UF3 (in melt) + F (tracked by the element chemical potential)
ln[ x(U⁴⁺) / x(U³⁺) ] = (μ_F − μ_F°) / RT

R is the gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K). T is the absolute temperature. μ_F° is a reference value fixed by the melt composition and the database. The log of the redox ratio is linear in the fluorine chemical potential.

Runμ_F (kJ/mol)ln[x(U⁴⁺)/x(U³⁺)] measured

At T = 900 K, RT = 7483 J/mol. A least-squares fit of ln(ratio) against μ_F over these three points gives a slope of 1.31×10⁻⁴ mol/J. The predicted slope is 1/RT = 1.336×10⁻⁴ mol/J. The fitted value and the predicted value agree to within 2%. The remaining difference comes from the melt not being an ideal solution.

Segment 4

Redox state over a temperature range

13 points, 800 K to 1400 K, in 50 K steps, computed in about 4.8 seconds. U(IV)/U(III) = 1.000 at every temperature, because F = 135.5 mol sets v̄ = 3.5 at every temperature. The mass balance does not depend on temperature.

Output structure

Two points matter for code that reads the JSON output of a molten salt phase.

  1. The MSFL phase block has no key named "species." It has keys named "cations," "anions," and "quadruplets" instead.
  2. Some cation names are eight characters long in the database and get truncated to eight characters in the output: U[CN=VII instead of U[CN=VII], and Be[Dimer instead of Be[Dimer]. A name ending in [Dimer represents two atoms bound as one unit.

Segment 5

Corrosion as a function of redox state

The next three runs add 0.01 mole of chromium metal to the same salt. Chromium stands in for a structural alloy in contact with the molten salt. Only the fluorine mass balance changes between the three runs, exactly as in Segment 4. Chromium corrosion in a fluoride melt proceeds by a reaction with the uranium redox buffer.

Cr (metal) + 2 UF4 (in melt) ⇌ CrF2 (in melt) + 2 UF3 (in melt) Cr (metal) + 3 UF4 (in melt) ⇌ CrF3 (in melt) + 3 UF3 (in melt)

Each reaction fixes an equilibrium constant, K₂ for the first and K₃ for the second, in terms of mole fractions.

K2 = x(Cr²⁺) / [ x(UF4)/x(UF3) ]² K3 = x(Cr³⁺) / [ x(UF4)/x(UF3) ]³

The column x(Cr²⁺) ÷ ratio² stays close to 3.3–3.9×10⁻⁷ across three runs that span five orders of magnitude in the UF₄/UF₃ ratio. A least-squares fit of log[x(Cr²⁺)] against log(ratio) gives a slope of 2.02. A matching fit for x(Cr³⁺) gives a slope of 3.04. Dissolved chromium rises by about nine orders of magnitude from the most reducing run to the most oxidizing run. It rises as a power of the uranium redox ratio, with the exact exponent the reaction chemistry predicts. This relation is the kind of quantity a coupled corrosion model needs as a boundary condition, covered in a separate session.

Segment 6

Off-gassing and the bubble point

The next two runs add small amounts of cesium and iodine to the same base salt. Cesium and iodine stand in for two volatile fission products. A gas phase becomes stable only when the total vapor pressure the melt would exert, at the given temperature and composition, reaches the system pressure. The pressure at which this first happens is the bubble point.

Segment 6

What this result does not state

  • This is the equilibrium vapor composition at that pressure and temperature. It is not a release rate.
  • The program does not compute how fast a species crosses from the liquid to the gas, or how fast the gas phase leaves the system.
  • Getting from this equilibrium composition to an actual off-gas rate needs a transport model. That model, and how it uses this equilibrium result as an input, is the subject of a separate session.
Segment 7

Summary

  1. The input to a Thermochimica calculation is temperature, pressure, and the mass balance of each element. The output is the stable phases, the amount of each, and the speciation inside each phase.
  2. A stoichiometric molten salt is an under-determined system for its individual element chemical potentials. Report species chemical potentials instead. Use a small perturbation or the fuzzy stoichiometry option if an element chemical potential is required.
  3. The halide mass balance sets the redox state of a multivalent element. The redox state sets the corrosion rate of a structural metal, through an equilibrium reaction with an integer reaction order confirmed directly from program output.
  4. This is an equilibrium calculation. It does not give a rate. A rate comes from a transport model, coupled to Thermochimica. That coupling is the subject of a separate session.
